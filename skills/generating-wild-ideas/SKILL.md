---
name: generating-wild-ideas
description: Use when David asks for ideas, options, a pitch, or "what could I build", or when proposing approaches to an open design question.
---

Fix the judge before generating anything, because ideas judged by loose standards drift toward whatever was easiest to think of. Write down what a winning idea must do, taken from David's own words, the project's handover or taste doc if one exists, and the constraints of the stack he's in, and don't relax those criteria later to let a favourite through. Novelty is not on the list; an idea earns its place by being surprising and by passing the judge, never by being strange alone.

Clear out the obvious first. Write the first five ideas that come to mind and mark them banned, since they're the ones any agent would give, along with anything in the ideas log that David has already rejected. Then generate from deliberately different directions, at least one idea from each:
- Invert: do the opposite of the default, or make the weakness the feature.
- Remove: what if the core part didn't exist, or the work never needed to happen?
- Steal: take a mechanism from an unrelated field (games, biology, logistics, music, finance) and port it.
- Scale: the same thing at 100× or 0.01×, for one user or a million.
- Flip a constraint: assume the hard limit is gone, or add a brutal new one.
- Collide: combine two things that don't belong together.
- Hostile user: design it for someone who hates the current version.

For each idea, write one line on why it would work and one on what would kill it, because an idea without a mechanism is a mood. Then judge honestly. Score every idea against the criteria, cut most of them, and if an idea is a known technique, say so by name, since rediscovering something is fine but passing it off as new isn't. When an idea survives for a different reason than the one you expected, say so and follow that reason, because it often points to a better idea next to it. When two ideas from the same direction both die, ban that direction for this round.

Pitch the best three, not twenty. For each, give the idea in one sentence, why it beats the obvious answer, what would kill it, and the cheapest test that would show whether it works by the end of the day. Pitch; don't quiz David on which direction interests him. Log every idea along with its verdict, plus David's reaction, so the next round starts from what he has already turned down instead of repeating it.
